UPS · September 2026

Still on the way

1Z41V8580116395843 · UPS Next Day Air · $127.84

For five days my passport sat somewhere inside UPS, and the tracking page told me the same three words every time I looked.

I went in expecting to find a broken product. What I found was a market built very carefully around this problem, leaving one gap open that nobody has a commercial reason to close.

Here is what I learned, and why the fix I landed on is smaller than I wanted it to be.

4 to 6 August

What I was doing while the screen said nothing

UPS never contacted me. Not one email, not one text, not one call. Every single thing I learned in these three days, I learned because I rang them and asked.

I refreshed that page more times than I can count. First thing in the morning. At lunch. Last thing at night. Again at two in the morning when I could not sleep and thought maybe the overnight sort had picked it up. Same three words. On the way.

I rang UPS three or four times a day on the 4th, the 5th and the 6th. Somewhere around a dozen calls. A different agent every time. I read out the tracking number every time. And every time, the person on the other end read me back the exact line I was already staring at.

By the 5th I had started calling twice in a row, on the theory that a different agent might have a different screen. They did not. Nobody I spoke to at UPS could see anything I could not see myself.

Every one of them told me it would arrive. On the evening of the 6th, around six o’clock, one of them finally said something different. She said it was now well past the delivery date and time for a next-day service, so the package was probably lost, and I should ring the UPS Store in Seattle and file a claim.

I rang the Seattle store. They were closing and there was nobody left to talk to me. And it would not have mattered if there had been. My husband had paid for the shipping, so the claim was his to file, not mine. I was the one with no passport, on a flight at two the next afternoon that I had to leave for by eleven. I was not a customer.

That evening I stopped being polite. I posted the whole thing on LinkedIn. I tweeted at UPS. Then I looked up email addresses and wrote directly to their Chief Executive, to the president of their US business, and to their Chief Commercial and Strategy Officer. I copied investor relations and the press office.

That worked. On the morning of the 7th the executive office forwarded my email down to UPS Corporate Customer Relations, and someone there took my case. She had my tracking number, my call history, everything. Her replies came from the general customerservice@ups.com mailbox, signed with a first name and an initial. One person, one shared inbox, and no other way to reach the case.

Which is worth saying plainly. Around a dozen calls to the number printed on the website got me a named human exactly zero times. Five emails, sent to people who were never meant to read them, got me one by the next morning. The product had no escalation path in it. The only one that existed was the one I built by going around the product entirely.

I flew home to Seattle that afternoon without my passport, on a TSA identity check at the airport that cost me $45 and a very long wait.

10 August

A stranger in Canada found my passport

A Senior Account Manager at UPS Canada had seen my LinkedIn post.

He messaged me. Over a weekend, in his own time, in a different country, for a customer who was not his, he went and looked at the back end.

Message from UPS Canada

They have found your passport. It is out for delivery at the Los Angeles address now.

MONDAY 10 AUGUST · 3:20PM PST

I did not know what to say back to him.

Because at that moment I had a live email thread running with the customer relations manager UPS had assigned me on the 7th. She had my case. She had every detail. And she knew nothing, because it was the end of her day and she had gone home.

A stranger in another country, with no reason to help me and nothing to gain from it, knew where my passport was. The person officially handling my case did not.

Thirty minutes later my phone rang. A supervisor from the UPS Los Angeles facility had driven to the delivery address herself, with my passport, and she was standing outside it.

Now look at the log again. The first thing UPS’s own system recorded that day was at 4:05pm, and what it recorded was that the receiver was not available. By then two people had already worked it out between them. A man in Canada had messaged me at 3:20, and a UPS supervisor had already driven the passport to the address herself.

The system wrote nothing for three quarters of an hour after he told me. And when it finally did write something, it was wrong. Then it wrote two more things, and those were wrong too.

Nobody had told me she was coming. I was in Seattle. I had flown home three days earlier.

So I had to ask her to send it back. New tracking number on the 13th. It reached Seattle on the 17th, two weeks after it was posted. UPS refunded the $127.84 and the $45 on the 25th, after a customer relations manager and a long chain of emails.

Nothing in the product worked. What worked was a stranger on LinkedIn deciding, on his own weekend, to go and look.

They never lost it. They had it the whole time. They just never told me anything, including that they were bringing it.

The question

This is not a delivery problem

UPS is good at delivering. In December 2025 they ran at 97.2% on time, the best of the big US carriers, ahead of FedEx Express at 95.3% and USPS at 94.1%.

There were three separate moments where UPS knew something and said nothing. The package stopped moving. A supervisor was on her way with it. It was going back across the country. I found out about all three of them late, and two of them only because someone went looking on my behalf.

UPS is good at delivering and bad at telling you when it fails.

From those I picked out the 110 people who had paid for a guaranteed fast service, because they are the ones with a promise to point at.

The turn

Then I looked at who had already solved it

This is where the case study actually starts, because my first assumption was wrong.

This is a mature market. Narvar has been doing it since 2012 and reaches 125 million people. FedEx has built its own version. Retailers have been paying real money for it for over a decade.

Finding one

Everyone built the telling. Almost nobody built the noticing.

They are two different jobs. Noticing is realising something has gone wrong. Telling is letting the customer know. Every company above is very good at the second one.

Narvar

The relay

It watches the carrier’s feed and retells it in the shop’s voice, on the shop’s page, with a button attached. Instead of leaving for ups.com, you stay where you bought the thing.

Retailers love it because a tracking page gets opened three or four times per order, and every one of those visits used to belong to the carrier. Now the shop keeps the traffic, the branding, and the chance to sell you something else.

The catch

It can only repeat what the carrier writes down. Silence in, silence out. It would have told me nothing, beautifully.

parcelLab

The forecaster

The most advanced of the group. It reads live carrier performance, past shipping patterns and regional data, and flags packages it thinks will run late before the carrier admits anything.

FedEx thought enough of it to put parcelLab’s technology inside their own product in February 2026, rather than spend years building an equivalent.

The catch

A prediction is a guess. They claim to catch 90% of delays at over 85% accuracy, which also means roughly one flag in seven is wrong. Warn people about parcels that were fine, often enough, and they stop reading the warnings.

And parcelLab is looking for packages that are slow. Not packages that have gone quiet. Mine never looked late. It looked completely normal. There was just nothing there.

A heart monitor does not beep every time the heart beats. It beeps when the beats stop.
Finding two

Every single one of them needs a shop

Narvar, parcelLab, ClickPost, AfterShip, and even FedEx’s own fdx. All of them require a retailer on the other end who has paid for the service.

A passport posted between two people is not a retail shipment. Nobody had bought anything on my behalf, so nobody told me anything, and there was no product in existence that would have.

The one with the data has no reason to speak. The one with the reason to speak has no data.

The carrier can see the package stop moving and has no commercial reason to advertise its own failures. The shop would happily say something and cannot see anything until the carrier writes it down first.

That is not a gap in the market. It is a gap between two parties, and it stays open because neither one is motivated to close it.

The fix

So what would it actually take

A rule that fires when nothing happens.

01
The delivery date shown to the customer has passed. Captured at shipping, not read live. When things go wrong UPS deletes the estimated date and replaces it with “we are working to get you a new delivery date”. A rule reading the live screen would watch the system erase its own deadline and never fire.
AND
02
No scan for a set gap. Twelve hours on next day services, twenty four on two day. Late but still moving is just late. It gets a new date and nothing else.

Both conditions together are what separate a delay from a disappearance. Either one on its own generates noise.

Facts only. It never says lost, missing, or that UPS cannot find it, because UPS does not know any of those things. And once a package is flagged, movement becomes news too. If a supervisor is driving to your address with it, that is exactly the moment to tell you.

What I cut after feedback. A case page, a named owner, a promised callback time, and a claim button. Every one of those needs a real person at a real facility to own it, which across hundreds of buildings is a hiring decision. In logistics what gets built is what is operationally doable, not what looks good on a screen.

Where I would launch it. The problem is universal, the launch is not. All 110 of my cases are people who paid for a fast service, so that is as far as my evidence reaches. The signal is cleaner there too: air packages get scanned several times a day, while ground moves in long hops and twelve hours of silence on ground is just a Tuesday. Start on the guaranteed air services, tune the gap against how often flagged packages turn up fine, then widen.

Why it has to be the carrier. A UPS agent already applied this rule on 6 August. She did it in her head, two days after the date had passed, using a number she invented on the call, only because I rang for the tenth time, and then she said “lost”, which is the one word a carrier’s lawyers will never put on a screen. I am not asking UPS to invent judgement it does not have. And Narvar cannot do this at all, because Narvar needs a shop to have paid.

Honestly

What this is actually worth

My own case is a good illustration of the problem and a weak illustration of the solution.

I was always going to fly home on the 7th. The physical outcome was fixed no matter what anyone told me. What this buys me is a day and a half of knowing, and not spending three days on the phone. That is real. It is not dramatic.

It will sometimes be wrong, and I should say so.A rule that fires on silence will occasionally fire on a package that was fine. A scan gets missed, a van runs late, and the message goes out to someone whose parcel turns up that afternoon. Annoying. And if it happens often enough people stop reading the messages, which is worse than never sending them. That is exactly why the rule needs both conditions rather than one, and why the gap is a dial rather than a number.
In year one this might cost UPS money.Telling people their package has stopped is more likely to make them ring than stop them ringing. And more people finding out means more claims, because today a lot of people never find out and never claim. Both are real.
What saves it is that it is almost free.A rule over data UPS already holds. No new tracking, no new staff. Cheap enough that an unproven upside still clears the bar, and cheap enough to run in one region and switch off.
For most customers the value is small.Knowing earlier does not get you your parcel. You still wait. Some people would rather know. Others just worry sooner.
For a few, it is everything.A passport before a flight. Medication. A filing deadline. A part for a machine that is down. This is insurance: a tiny cost spread over everything, for a rare but large payoff. Nobody argues insurance is good value on an average day.
This is a small feature. It will not move UPS’s numbers much. It matters enormously to a small number of people on the worst day of their shipping year.
Measurement

How I would know it worked

The main number. How often UPS tells the customer before the customer asks. Today that is close to zero.

The one that matters most in my own case. Repeat contacts per exception. I made around a dozen calls in three days and learned nothing from any of them.

The guardrail. Claims filed per exception, before and after. And how often a flagged package turns up within a few hours of being flagged.

The number I would ask UPS for on day one. After a shipper has a package go missing, does their volume drop over the next three months? They have it. I do not. It decides whether this protects the network growth they are already paying for, or whether it is only a decent thing to do.

Revisions

Where I changed my answer

I checked whether this was already solved. It was.Four companies and a rival carrier had built it. Doing that check is what produced the actual finding: every one of them built it for shops, and none of them for the person waiting.
I cut the best-looking screen I had.My first design named an owner and promised an update by 5pm. A mentor pointed out that if nobody is really assigned, that screen does the exact thing I was complaining about. It promises something the system cannot keep. So it went.
I corrected my own public claim.I had said UPS lost my passport. They did not. They had it the whole time, a supervisor drove it to the door, and nobody told me any of it.
I marked my own fix down.I twice valued it higher than it deserved. Checked against my real timeline, it would not have got my passport to me one hour sooner. What it buys is three days of phone calls I never make, and a day and a half of knowing. That is the honest number.
The next one

The bigger thing I noticed and did not solve

My husband paid for the shipping. So UPS’s contract was with him. The refund was his to claim and the apology was his to receive.

I was the one with no passport, three days on the phone, and a $45 check at an airport counter.

The money and the harm sat with different people, and I could not even file the claim.

That is not a quirk of my story. It is how the whole system is built. You buy from a shop, the shop pays the carrier, the carrier breaks it, the carrier pays the shop. The person actually standing at the wrong end of it is not in that conversation at all.

It is also the shape of the bigger problems in this industry. Returns fraud works precisely because the person who loses the money and the person who caused the loss are never the same person.

That is worth a second case study, properly researched. It is bigger than this one.
